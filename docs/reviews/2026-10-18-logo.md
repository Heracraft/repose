# The logo: studies and the mark that came out of them (2026-10-18)

The owner on the I-363 mark: "our logo is shit, go crazy and design
something new and simple". On the first answer to that: "kinda looks like
opencode". On the second: "the first one was the only real one in terms
of quality, all the other ones are shit." On the third: "quality is a bad
metric, lets create something beautiful and distinct." On the fourth:
"ugly as shit" -- and then the brief that produced the mark: combine the
page's own signs, consider what the product is and why devs will care;
then duplication, shadows, mirrors, cells replicating, clones formed out
of a body's own material, "we want to show the duplication still
happening but almost perfect". This records what was drawn, how it was
judged, what those notes cost, and why the mark in `Logo.svelte` today
(I-779) is the one that survived.

## How each round was judged

Every candidate was drawn as flat rectangles on a 100 grid, in the
foundation's colours only (the text's ink, `--sh-accent`, and for the
first round the two greys the old mark used), and rendered at 72, 28, 24
and 16px, in both schemes, beside the Noto Serif 600 wordmark. The 16px
cut was also rasterised and blown up 7x with no smoothing, so the tab's
pixel grid could be read rather than guessed at. Then the finalists were
put in a real 57px header row at 1440 and in a browser-tab mock.

That test, and not the 72px drawing, is what cut each round. It is the
same rule `LANDING.md` keeps for the page: a visual is evidence only at
the size a visitor sees it.

## What the old mark failed

I-363's cross has stems of 4 units on the 100 grid. At 28px that is
1.1px, at 24px under a pixel, and the three blocks hugging the crossing
fall below a pixel each. On the pixel grid the mark is a grey smudge with
a blue dot; the tab needed a second drawing at 2.5 times the stem weight
to say anything at all. Two drawings for one mark is also two things to
keep in step, and they had already drifted in the blocks' sizes.

So the first rule of the new one: nothing in the drawing thinner than
about a sixth of it, and one drawing for every size.

## Two answers that were sent back

**The ring.** A square ring in ink with the accent block astride its
lower-right corner. It passed every size test and the owner read it in
one line: it looks like opencode. It does. opencode's mark is a thick
square-cornered O whose lower counter is filled, and opencode is the
nearest neighbour this product has, so a ring, a frame, or a block inside
a box reads as that product and not this one. That cut a family, not one
drawing: the box with the block inside it, the block on the floor of the
box, the block astride the corner. **The mark encloses nothing.**

**The cradle.** A block at rest in the crook of an L. It cleared the
neighbour bar and lost what the owner had liked in the ring: "the first
one was the only real one in terms of quality, all the other ones are
shit."

To find out what "quality" meant here rather than guess at it, 56 peer
marks (simple-icons silhouettes: the cloud-dev and developer-tool field,
from vercel and linear to gitpod, railway, render, terraform and nixos)
were rendered in one grid at 56px and again at 16px. The split is plain
at the small size. What holds is **one connected silhouette with mass and
a single internal cut**: framer, remix, render, gitpod, vercel, prisma,
stripe. What falls apart is a mark made of separate pieces: pnpm, consul,
nx, posthog, slack. The ring is in the first class. The cradle, an L and
a block with daylight between them, is in the second. **One silhouette,
with the accent a region of it, flush and never detached.**

## The third note: a rulebook is not a drawing

The third answer was a square block with a square slot and a square key.
It obeys both rules above and it is a diagram of them. "Quality is a bad
metric, lets create something beautiful and distinct."

So the next round was drawn for presence, with one constraint lifted: the
mark may carry a gesture the rest of the system does not have. The
foundation's radius scale stops at 4px and every page draws rectangles,
but the landing already keeps a sphere, and a logo earns a curve. Ten
marks: an arch on two legs, a dog-ear, a quarter disc at rest in a
corner, a scooped side, a chamfer, the owner's cross given mass, a half
disc on a slab, a square cut on the slant, a leaf of two arcs, and a
round-ended machined slot.

The round end won, and then it suggested the mark: if the slot is cut
with a round end, the piece that came out of it has that same end. Draw
both, one above the other, and the mark says the whole thing — a cut, and
what the cut produced, kept.

## The rounds

Round one, fifteen marks across every idea worth a try: twin boxes, a
corner moved out of a square, brackets around a block, a monolith with a
lit window, offset bars, a block straddling a rule, a box inside a box, a
square with a lit corner, a stack of units, a rising staircase, a square
split on the diagonal, a block on a plinth, a doorway, and a hollow slot
with its block beside it. Half died at 16px: the stack reads as a
hamburger menu, the staircase as a bar chart, the straddled rule as a
dash, the plinth as a floating square. Rounds two to four refined the
nested box, which the neighbour test then threw out.

Round five started again with enclosures barred: an interlock, a carry, a
twin, a shift, a cradle, brackets, an arch, a plinth, a wedge and a step.
Round six tuned the cradle, which the quality test then threw out.

Rounds eight to ten were drawn under both rules: one silhouette, with
mass, no enclosure. A step seam, a notch, a shear, a stair, a slot, a
fused L, a tromino, a bevel, a keystone, offset halves. Three held in a
real 57px header row: the notch, the keystone (a solid square with one
corner in the accent) and the step seam. The notch went forward, and came
back as a rulebook.

Rounds eleven to sixteen were drawn for presence, with the curve allowed.
The round-ended slot carried it, the key took the slot's own profile, and
the last rounds settled the proportions: 16 of band, 36 of slot, 36 of
key, each cut 36 deep on a radius of 18. The keystone is still the
runner-up to it.

## The brief that worked

Four rounds were spent on form: a ring, a cradle, a square slot, a keyed
slot. Each answered the last note and none of them was about the product.
The fifth note named the subject instead: duplication, shadows, mirrors,
cells, the clone that forms out of a body's own material in an anime --
and the condition that makes it a picture rather than a diagram, *the
duplication still happening, but almost perfect*.

That is the product. `repose run` replicates the working state of your
laptop onto a machine that is not your laptop, and the moment worth
drawing is the one just before the copy is finished.

Twelve drawings of it: a shadow a hair out of register, a shadow pulling
away, mitosis with a narrow neck and with a thick one, a bud, a bud grown
nearly to size, a mirror pair, a mirror with a piece still missing, a
copy three quarters made, a block and its shadow at two distances, and
twins overprinted. The ones with two separate bodies (mitosis, the bud)
read as a Venn diagram or a planet and its moon at header size. The ones
where the copy is damaged rather than unfinished read as damage. What
survived is the pair that is almost in register.

Then the seam. A copy that touches its original is a drop shadow; a copy
with a gap all the way round is a second object, and the gap is what says
"still happening". The accent shape is therefore not a disc: it is what a
second disc has cleared of the first disc grown by the seam, drawn as two
arcs. The last rounds set the three numbers that follow from that: the
original's radius, the copy's (smaller, because it is not finished), and
the seam.

## The mark

A disc of 34 at 38,38 is the original. A second disc, 32 at 63,63, is the
copy. The accent shape is what the copy has cleared of the original grown
by the seam of 5:

    <circle cx="38" cy="38" r="34"/>
    <path d="M34.1 76.8A32 32 0 0 0 76.8 34.1A39 39 0 0 1 34.1 76.8Z"/>

The crescent is 20 wide at its widest and the seam is 5, which at 16px is
3.2 and 0.8 pixels: the copy still reads, and so does the gap. The copy
is smaller than the original because it is not finished yet; evening them
up is the one change that breaks the mark.

## The runners-up, and why they lost

| Mark | Why not |
|---|---|
| Mitosis, two discs joined by a neck | A Venn diagram below about 48px. |
| A bud growing off the body | A planet and its moon. |
| Mirror pair across a seam | It is the landing's halves sign, which already means the localhost forward. |
| The copy with a quarter still missing | Reads as damage, not as progress. |
| Block and its shadow, a step behind | Clean, and it is a drop shadow on a card. |
| Box with the block astride its corner | Reads as opencode. |
| Block at rest in the crook of an L | Two separate pieces; dies at a tab's size. |
| Square block, square slot, square key | Obeys the rules and reads as a diagram of them. |
| Round-ended slot and its key | Beautiful, and about nothing the product does. |

## Where the mark is

`Logo.svelte` (every header and the footer), `static/favicon.svg` and
`favicon.png`, and `ops/logto/logo-mark.svg` for the Logto sign-in page
and the sign-in emails. The icon links carry `?v=3`, so a browser holding
the old sketch drops it.
